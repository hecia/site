On campus, the rumor is well established. Students pass it on like an urban legend: finance internships are over, funds have stopped hiring, an LBO model now gets built in eight seconds, and we'll all end up retraining in artisanal pottery.

Thanks to Antoine Colson for inviting me to IPEM Global. 6,200 attendees, including 1,550 LPs announcing more than $100bn to allocate over the next twelve months. The format stays tight, and that is what makes the event valuable. There is nothing quite like it in Europe. One thing to complain about: the coffee queue. But I think it's deliberate — that's where I had my best conversations.

I spent the day putting the question to the people who actually hire. Because "will AI replace analysts" makes a good headline, but it's not a useful question.

The real question comes in two parts. **If the machine now does what used to be given to a junior, what is left for a junior to do? And how does someone become a partner in fifteen years, without having put in the hours that built the judgment?**

## Part 1. Three families of funds

One day of interviews is enough to see the split.

The observers. The topic is being watched, discussed internally, but nothing has actually moved: the tools already in place, a shared consumer subscription, and the belief that the job is too specific to automate. That's especially true at large firms, where any change has to go through IT, compliance, and a budget. The interest is real; it hasn't yet turned into action.

The equipped. They started from specific use cases: one tool for data rooms, another for meeting notes, a third for market monitoring. Each piece works. What's missing is the layer above: nobody is in charge of making it all hold together. This is the largest family.

The AI-native. Rare. They're not asking which tool to bolt onto what already exists — they're starting from scratch: what would an investment firm built today actually look like. The question applies as much to investing as to the middle and back office (KYC, LP reporting, compliance, fund administration), which is where the hours and the cost base actually sit. And it changes how sourcing is approached in the first place, because processing data at scale has become easy. This is exactly where funds are not on equal footing.

In VC, sourcing means spotting a signal in the noise of several thousand companies, and the machine excels at that. In mid cap, sourcing means "the investment banker in Lyon who knows an owner planning to sell in eighteen months," and no model is going to have coffee with him in your place.

## Part 2. So, what do we actually do?

This is where the campus rumor falls apart. The sentence I heard the most, almost word for word:

> "I'm looking for almost the same profiles."

**Note the "almost." It's doing all the work.**

The same, first. No engineers, no prompt engineers, no trendy hybrid profile. Nobody told me they were looking for anything other than a good analyst. You can put the pottery retraining plan away.

And then that "almost," which isn't about what you're expected to know, but about the point at which you're expected to become useful.

Before, an intern spent six months learning to produce output. Today, producing is the easy part, and everyone knows it, including whoever reads your work. What you're now asked to do is know whether what came out is correct. That's not the same skill, and it's a much harder one: you have to recognize the right answer before you've reached it.

The profile on the rise, then, isn't the one who knows how to produce. It's the one who iterates, who questions what they just produced, and who improves faster because they've stopped wasting three days on formatting.

The real question remains. If juniors no longer put in those hours, how do we build the partners of 2040? A partner is someone who has built three hundred models and can sense, looking at a business plan, that something is off, without being able to say right away what. **That intuition is bought with hours. Remove the hours, and you remove the intuition.**

I didn't get a clear answer. What kept coming back: the partner role will itself change shape, so the point isn't to reproduce the old path. And several people told me this is a good time to be starting out, because the people coming in now will help write what the job becomes.

## And the part no machine takes

This job is about convincing an institution to trust you with its money for ten years, and convincing an owner to sell you the company his grandfather founded. Two conversations where the question is never the quality of your model.

It's a job built on experience, in the literal sense: deals seen, meetings, a network, mistakes. That doesn't download.

**So no, finance internships are not dead.** The job is changing shape, and that's actually good news: there have rarely been so many open questions for someone just starting out.

The coffee queue, on the other hand, will remain a human problem.

*Hugo Roullet, Vice-President of HEC IA*
